# Chapter Two: The Lyre — a proposal, revised

A proposal to react to, not a plan. Nothing here is built. Scripture: 1 Samuel 16:14–23, with 17:15 for the ending. Quotes are from the World English Bible, checked 16 November 2026.

## What changed in this revision

- The lyre is now learned in Chapter One (`CHAPTER_ONE_PLAN.md`, the smaller version). He arrives here already able to play; this chapter makes the playing harder and gives it a new use, on people.
- The first gift is no longer the lyre itself.
- Bethlehem was left out of Chapter One's smaller version. It is built first here, for Section 1, and can be taken back into Chapter One afterwards.
- The flock he hands over is now the nine the player has kept all through Chapter One.
- The twelve film shots and four paintings are collected, not queued.

## What the chapter is about

Chapter One asked: will he go after the one that is lost? Chapter Two asks something harder for a boy who has just been anointed: can he serve the king whose place he has been promised, and do him good?

There are almost no beasts in it. If the chapter is built like Chapter One with more lyre, it will feel empty. So the lyre has to be the thing the player does, the way the staff and sling were.

## The lyre in this chapter

**What the player already knows.** Hold a key to draw a phrase; a line rises and falls like a breath; release at the top and the phrase lands. Three phrases settle the sheep. That is being tried in Chapter One's first tranche; if you change it there, it changes here.

**What is new here.**

- **He plays on his feet.** In Chapter One he only plays seated. Here he learns to play standing and walking, so he can lead an animal while he plays.
- **It works on people.** An angry gatekeeper, a tired steward, a troubled king. People take more than sheep and do not stay calm for long.
- **It is opposed.** A donkey shies at a noise and the breath line jumps. The king's torment comes in waves that speed the line up. Playing well under pressure is the skill.
- **A phrase can be lost.** With sheep, a missed breath only costs time. With the king, the shadow grows.

**Why this and not a rhythm game.** A family audience, a serious tone, and a controller or keyboard with no timing calibration.

## Going back

The Walking Song and the Night Song open things in Chapter One's places. Animals that fled him then can be led now: the wild goats on the crag (invention here, though the "rocks of the wild goats" are named in 1 Samuel 24:2), the ewe's lost twin, a jackal bitch with pups that leaves the path when played to. That gives the Chapter One map new reasons to revisit.

## Scripture, and what is not scripture

- 16:19–20: "Send me David your son, who is with the sheep." Jesse sends "a donkey loaded with bread, and a bottle of wine, and a young goat".
- 16:18: before he arrives he is called "skillful in playing, a mighty man of valor, a man of war, prudent in speech". Chapter One's fights are what earn those words.
- 16:16, 16:21 and 17:15: as quoted in the table. 16:23: "David took the harp, and played with his hand; so Saul was refreshed, and was well". The World English Bible says "harp"; "lyre" is the usual reading of the Hebrew word.
- 17:1: the Philistines "gathered together at Socoh, which belongs to Judah". Scripture; only the men running south are invented.
- 17:20: "left the sheep with a keeper". That verse is from the day he goes to Elah; using a keeper here too is a small invention.
- The road: Judges 19:10–12 has a traveller pass Jebus, refuse "the city of a foreigner that is not of the children of Israel", and go on to Gibeah. Jebus stays Jebusite until David takes it (2 Samuel 5:6).
- Gibeah: Saul's house was there (10:26; 15:34, "Gibeah of Saul"). The text does not say where David played, and it describes no fortress. The fortress, gate and yard follow the remains usually identified at Tell el-Ful, whose identification and date are disputed.
- Invention: what happens on the road, the gate, the steward, finding the young man of 16:18 at the gate, the king's shadow on the wall, the tasks about the fortress, the drill, lamps at night, the men running south, and the king's shield and spear (16:21 says only "armor bearer").

**A known puzzle in the text.** After Goliath, Saul asks "whose son is this youth?" and Abner "can't tell" (17:55–58), though David has already played for Saul and carried his armour. Readers have long explained this in different ways: one common reading is that Saul is asking about David's father and family, not who David is. The game follows the order of the text as it stands, and the journal should say this is a puzzle, not hide it.

**The older Greek text.** The Septuagint, the Greek translation made before the time of Jesus, has a much shorter version of these chapters in its oldest manuscripts (Codex Vaticanus). It lacks 17:12–31, 41, 48b, 50, 55–58 and 18:1–6a, 10–11, 12b, 17–19, 21b, 29b–30: 39 of the 88 verses of 1 Samuel 16–18, about 44 percent of chapters 17 and 18. The question "whose son is this youth?" is one of the missing passages, so in the Greek there is no puzzle: David is the harpist and armour-bearer throughout. Scholars disagree about why. Some hold the Greek translator cut the text to smooth it; others hold he translated a shorter Hebrew text that is older than ours. Source: Emanuel Tov, "The Composition of 1 Samuel 16–18 in Light of the Septuagint", in *The Greek and Hebrew Bible* (Brill, 1999), chapter 23, note 2. The World English Bible, and so this game, follows the longer Hebrew text.

This touches more than the puzzle. Also missing from the Greek: Jonathan's soul "knit with the soul of David" (18:1, question 5), and Saul's spear (18:10–11, question 6). Chapter One's plan uses 17:12 ("eight sons") and 17:28 ("those few sheep"), which are missing too.

**The shield.** At Elah, David takes off Saul's armour and sword: "I can't go with these; for I have not tested them" (17:39). So he should not leave this chapter skilled in armour. The Shield Arm is kept to carrying and raising a shield for the king, not wearing armour or fighting in it.

## Things I am unsure of, and need you for

1. **The lyre's feel.** Settled by playing Chapter One's first tranche. If breath-and-release is wrong there, say so before this chapter is built on it.
2. **Which words he plays.** In Chapter One each line is Psalm 23. Here, does he play that psalm to the king, or lines from other psalms, or no words at all? I would have him play Psalm 23 the first time, because the player made it.
3. **How much fighting?** As proposed there are wolves on the road and a shield drill, and nothing else. Is that too little for the game you want?
4. **How is Saul's torment shown?** A growing shadow is restrained and never shows a spirit. Is that the right tone for a family audience?
5. **Jonathan.** He is already named and a soldier before David comes (1 Samuel 13:2, chapter 14, and 14:49 lists him among Saul's sons), and he was at Gibeah. But David first meets him only after Goliath: "the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David" (18:1). Seen at a distance in the yard, or held back entirely? I would show him at a distance, never speaking to David.
6. **The spear.** Saul throws it in 18:10–11, after Goliath. Hinting at it here is invention. Keep the hint, or leave the chapter warm?
7. **Length.** Chapter One will be about 70 to 85 minutes after the smaller version. The same here, or shorter?

## What I would do differently from Chapter One

- Decide each section's reason and its gift before building it. Chapter One's abilities first appeared with no cause, and had to be rebuilt.
- Make every film introduce who is on screen.
- Build each section at its full length the first time.
- Have you play each section as it is finished, not all of them at the end.
